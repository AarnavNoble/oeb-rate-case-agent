## cmp-2. What return on equity and capital structure (debt/equity split) does each utility use in its application?

_Tool calls: 4, citations: 8 (0 unverified), cases cited: EB-2023-0195, EB-2026-0164_

Both utilities use the OEB's formula-based ROE. They differ on capital structure: Toronto Hydro keeps the standard 60/40 debt/equity split, and Hydro One asks for 55/45 on Transmission and Distribution.

**Toronto Hydro (EB-2023-0195, distribution, 2025–2029)**
- **Capital structure:** The settlement sets a deemed 60% debt and 40% common equity. The 60% debt is 4% short-term and 56% long-term. This is unchanged from current rates and consistent with the OEB's 2009 cost of capital report [EB-2023-0195 / THESL_Settlement Proposal_20240816 / p.22]. The OEB's partial decision on the settlement records the same terms [EB-2023-0195 / dec_order_Partial_THESL_20241112_signed / p.70].
- **ROE:** The application used a forecast 2025 ROE of 9.36%. That came from the OEB's formula, using September 2022 data [EB-2023-0195 / Exhibit 5 / p.2]. The settlement says the 2025–2029 ROE will be set from the 2025 ROE the OEB publishes in Q4 2024, so 9.36% was only a placeholder [EB-2023-0195 / THESL_Settlement Proposal_20240816 / p.22]. The filings I retrieved don't show the final 2025 figure.
- **Debt cost:** The settled long-term debt rate is 3.91% [EB-2023-0195 / THESL_Settlement Proposal_20240816 / p.22].
- **2026–2029:** Toronto Hydro will implement whatever the OEB decides in the generic cost of capital proceeding (EB-2024-0063) [EB-2023-0195 / dec_order_Partial_THESL_20241112_signed / p.70].

**Hydro One (EB-2026-0164, Transmission and Distribution, 2028–2032)**
- **Capital structure:** Hydro One proposes 45% common equity and 55% debt for both Transmission and Distribution, starting in 2028. The debt is 51% long-term and 4% short-term. This replaces the current 40% equity [EB-2026-0164 / Exhibit A / p.57]. Its consultant, Concentric, recommended 45–50% equity, with 47.5% as the midpoint. Hydro One chose the low end of that range [EB-2026-0164 / Exhibit F / p.3] [EB-2026-0164 / Exhibit A / p.221].
- **ROE:** Hydro One uses 9.11% as a placeholder for 2028–2032. That is the OEB's 2026 formula ROE, and Hydro One will update it when the OEB publishes the 2028 parameters [EB-2026-0164 / Exhibit F / p.7].
- **Hydro One Sault Ste. Marie (HOSSM):** This is a separate Hydro One entity in the application. It keeps the standard 40% equity and 60% debt (4% short-term, 56% long-term). Its 2028 table uses a 9.11% ROE [EB-2026-0164 / Exhibit A / p.61].

## cmp-3. How does each utility justify its pace of investment in end-of-life or deteriorating assets? Name the main asset classes each one highlights.

_Tool calls: 5, citations: 20 (0 unverified), cases cited: EB-2023-0195, EB-2026-0164_

Both utilities justify their pace with condition and risk, not age alone. Toronto Hydro says it sets the pace to stop the backlog of old assets from growing. Hydro One says it targets the highest-risk poor-condition assets and limits the growth of that population. I did not look at Hydro One's transmission line programs (conductors, towers) in detail, so that part of its list is incomplete.

## Toronto Hydro (EB-2023-0195, distribution, 2025–2029)

**How it justifies the pace**
- **Backlog stabilization.** About 25% of its assets are past useful life, and another 11% will reach that point by 2030. That is two percentage points worse than the 2018 projection. It argues that a "significant proactive renewal program" is needed to keep the backlog from growing. It adds that each percentage point of the backlog represents hundreds of millions of dollars of future need [EB-2023-0195 / Exhibit 2B / p.20].
- **Condition and consequence, not age alone.** It says it does not replace assets just because they pass useful life. Replacement decisions use Asset Condition Assessment health scores and the consequence of failure. The assets-past-useful-life metric is only a directional indicator. It is used for pacing only where long-term spikes are expected, such as network units in 2030–2034 [EB-2023-0195 / Exhibit 2B / p.39] [EB-2023-0195 / Exhibit 2B / p.288].
- **Options analysis.** For most programs it compares reduced, moderate and accelerated paces and picks a middle option. For underground renewal, the selected option would take 20–25 years to eliminate direct-buried cable. The reduced option would take 30–35 years and leave more cable in service as it ages. The selected option is described as "the most reasonable balance" between reliability and cost [EB-2023-0195 / Exhibit 2B / p.281] [EB-2023-0195 / Exhibit 2B / p.280].
- **Legacy-asset targets.** Examples are eliminating box construction poles by 2026 and removing PCB-contaminated oil risk by 2025 [EB-2023-0195 / Exhibit 2B / p.41].

**Asset classes it highlights**
- Underground cable: direct-buried cable and paper- and asbestos-insulated lead-covered cable. For lead cable it plans to replace about 3.5% of the 985 km of paper-insulated cable and 5.3% of the 176 km of asbestos-insulated cable [EB-2023-0195 / Exhibit 2B / p.285].
- Underground transformers, switchgear and cable chambers. Of about 10,657 chambers, roughly 2,208 are past useful life [EB-2023-0195 / Exhibit 2B / p.299].
- Overhead assets: wood poles (about 9% showing material deterioration in 2022, forecast to reach 30% by 2029 without investment), pole-top transformers, overhead switches and conductors. Rear-lot and box construction are called out as legacy types [EB-2023-0195 / PWU_IRs_THESL_20240212 / p.13] [EB-2023-0195 / Exhibit 2B / p.41].
- Network units and vaults, and stations power transformers (it flags those operating beyond a target maximum age of 65 years) [EB-2023-0195 / Exhibit 2B / p.39].

## Hydro One (EB-2026-0164, 2028–2032, transmission and distribution)

**How it justifies the pace**
- **Risk-based, condition-driven.** Assets are treated as end-of-life when they have a high likelihood of failure with unacceptable consequences. That is distinct from the accounting "useful life." Equipment is replaced when it reaches end-of-life based on health metrics and performance history [EB-2026-0164 / Exhibit B1 / p.1683].
- **Trend evidence for poles.**
  - Poor-condition poles identified each year rose about 50%, from 10,000 in 2021 to about 15,000 over 2023–2025.
  - Pole-caused customer interruptions rose about 58% from 2020 to 2025 [EB-2026-0164 / Exhibit B3 / p.1207].
  - The proposed pace would hold the poor-condition pole population to about 135,000 in 2032. Without intervention it would exceed 200,000 [EB-2026-0164 / Exhibit B3 / p.1232].
  - The pace is also tied to cost-effective life extension (test and treat, refurbishment) and to highest-consequence-first prioritization [EB-2026-0164 / Exhibit B3 / p.1226].
- **Obsolescence as a driver.** Of its breaker fleet, 2,061 breakers (44%) are obsolete. About 545 (11%) are no longer vendor-supported, and about 329 connection-station breakers (9%) are in poor condition [EB-2026-0164 / Exhibit B / p.939].
- **Facilities.** Without more investment, the share of high-criticality buildings in poor condition is forecast to rise to 63% for OC Admin buildings and 38% for transmission station facilities by 2032. The proposed program would cut those shares to 2% and 20% [EB-2026-0164 / Exhibit B / p.549].

**Asset classes it highlights**
- Distribution: wood poles (the Pole Sustainment Program covers test and treat, refurbishment, replacement and clustered hardening). Also feeder lines (poles, conductor, hardware), where it favours integrated rebuilds of off-road sections [EB-2026-0164 / Exhibit B3 / p.1275]. Also distribution station breakers, where 72% of the fleet in the Hydro One rate zone is beyond its estimated service life [EB-2026-0164 / Exhibit B / p.93].
- Transmission stations: power transformers and autotransformers, circuit breakers (air-blast and first-generation SF6), protection and control equipment, instrument transformers and station service equipment [EB-2026-0164 / Exhibit B1 / p.2225] [EB-2026-0164 / Exhibit B / p.74].
- Transmission lines: conductors, for example the 90-year-old Gatineau Corridor [EB-2026-0164 / Exhibit B1 / p.1683].
- General plant: buildings (roofs, HVAC) and IT hardware at vendor end-of-support [EB-2026-0164 / Exhibit B / p.105].

## Comparability
- Toronto Hydro is a distribution-only utility, and it leans on the assets-past-useful-life percentage and on health-index bands (HI4 and HI5) to frame its backlog.
- Hydro One covers both transmission and distribution. It leans more on poor-condition counts, outage and customer-minute trends, and obsolescence.
- Their numbers measure different things (percent past useful life against counts of poor-condition assets), so they should not be compared directly.

## cmp-4. What rate-setting framework does each application propose (rebasing, annual adjustment formula, productivity factor, stretch factor, capital funding mechanism)?

_Tool calls: 5, citations: 11 (1 unverified), cases cited: EB-2023-0195, EB-2026-0164_

**Both utilities propose a Custom IR framework.** Each rebases in its first year, then adjusts revenue through a custom revenue cap index. They differ in how they set the productivity and stretch factors and in how the capital funding factor works.

**Toronto Hydro (EB-2023-0195), distribution, 2025–2029**
- **Rebasing and term:** The framework runs five years. Rates for 2025 are set on a rebasing basis, and the index applies to 2026–2029 [EB-2023-0195 / dec_order_Partial_THESL_20241112_signed / p.39].
- **Annual adjustment formula:** The Custom Revenue Cap Index is CRCI = I – X + RGF. I is the OEB's inflation parameter for each year. RGF is a revenue growth factor [EB-2023-0195 / dec_order_Partial_THESL_20241112_signed / p.39]. Toronto Hydro's own overview describes the RGF as escalating revenue so the utility can fund capital and operations in the outer years [EB-2023-0195 / Exhibit 1A / p.166]. Its evidence also proposes a custom labour index (the Conference Board of Canada Toronto salary and wages index) within the inflation factor [EB-2023-0195 / Exhibit 1A / p.166].
- **Productivity factor:** 0% in both Toronto Hydro's proposal and the settlement [EB-2023-0195 / dec_order_Partial_THESL_20241112_signed / p.39].
- **Stretch factor:**
  - Toronto Hydro proposed a 0.15% efficiency stretch factor, plus a 0.6% incremental "PIM" stretch factor that it could earn back through a Performance Incentive Mechanism [EB-2023-0195 / dec_order_Partial_THESL_20241112_signed / p.39].
  - The settlement replaces these with a 0.6% efficiency stretch factor and no PIM factor [EB-2023-0195 / dec_order_Partial_THESL_20241112_signed / p.39].
  - Toronto Hydro described its original combined X-factor as 0.75%, which it put at risk against scorecard performance [EB-2023-0195 / Exhibit 1B / p.473].
- **Capital funding:**
  - The RGF provides the incremental revenue needed to fund capital-related revenue requirement and operating costs [EB-2023-0195 / dec_order_Partial_THESL_20241112_signed / p.39].
  - The settlement adds a 0.3% incremental stretch factor on capital-related revenue requirement. It also sets the operating piece as OEB inflation plus a 0.41% growth factor tied to customer and peak demand growth [EB-2023-0195 / dec_order_Partial_THESL_20241112_signed / p.39].
  - The settlement also adds variance accounts: a revenue variance account, a demand-related capital variance account and a non-wires solutions variance account [EB-2023-0195 / dec_order_Partial_THESL_20241112_signed / p.39].

**Hydro One (EB-2026-0164), joint transmission and distribution, 2028–2032**
- **Rebasing and term:** The 2028 revenue requirement is set on a cost-of-service basis. Revenue requirements for 2029–2032 come from a Custom Revenue Cap Index [EB-2026-0164 / Exhibit A / p.67].
- **Annual adjustment formula:** RCI = I – X + G + IPD(OM&A) + C [EB-2026-0164 / Exhibit A / p.229].
  - I is the OEB's two-factor inflation index.
  - G is a growth factor.
  - IPD is an input price differential scaled to OM&A.
  - C is a custom capital factor.
  - The growth factor and IPD are new relative to the prior framework (EB-2021-0110) [EB-2026-0164 / Exhibit A / p.282].
- **Productivity factor (base):** The base is informed by Clearspring's industry total factor productivity (TFP) study. The X-factor is not allowed to go negative [EB-2026-0164 / Exhibit A / p.67].
  - Transmission's base is –2.30% [EB-2026-0164 / Exhibit A / p.393].
  - Distribution's base is –0.31% [EB-2026-0164 / Exhibit A / p.405].
- **Stretch factor:** These come from total cost benchmarking:
  - Transmission: 0.15% [EB-2026-0164 / Exhibit A / p.378].
  - Hydro One Sub-Transmission and Sub-Metering (HOSSM): 0.00% [EB-2026-0164 / Exhibit A / p.378].
  - Distribution: 0.60% [EB-2026-0164 / Exhibit A / p.405].
- **Resulting X-factor:**
  - Transmission: –2.30% + 0.15% sums to a negative number, so it is set at 0% [EB-2026-0164 / Exhibit A / p.393].
  - Distribution: –0.31% + 0.60% = 0.29%, applied cumulatively to both OM&A and capital-related revenue requirement [EB-2026-0164 / Exhibit A / p.405].
  - The tables show 0.00% for HOSSM [EB-2026-0164 / Exhibit A / p.230].
  - The X-factor is not updated annually during the term [EB-2026-0164 / Exhibit A / p.405].
- **Capital funding:**
  - The C-factor recovers the incremental capital-related revenue each year beyond what I – X + G provides. It is reduced by a 0.15% supplemental stretch factor on capital, for both transmission and distribution [EB-2026-0164 / Exhibit A / p.229].
  - The C-factor is updated annually for the current OEB inflation factors [EB-2026-0164 / Exhibit A / p.282].
  - Working capital is funded on an I – X basis rather than through the C-factor [EB-2026-0164 / Exhibit A / p.405].
  - Prepaid cloud computing amortization is added to the capital-related revenue requirement [EB-2026-0164 / Exhibit A / p.282].
  - Hydro One says a standard I – X index would underfund distribution by about $2.35B over 2029–2032 [ho-honi-appl-ex-a-admin p.279 (unknown document)].

**Comparability**
- Toronto Hydro's figures are for distribution only. Hydro One's cover transmission, HOSSM and distribution separately.
- Toronto Hydro's stretch factor applies to the whole revenue cap. Hydro One's stretch is part of X, plus a separate 0.15% supplemental stretch on capital.
- The Toronto Hydro figures above are the settlement terms. I have not checked whether the OEB approved the partial settlement in full.
- Hydro One's figures are proposals, and the 2029–2032 index values in its tables are pro forma [EB-2026-0164 / Exhibit A / p.230].

## cmp-5. What bill impact does each utility forecast for a typical residential customer in the first test year?

_Tool calls: 2, citations: 5 (0 unverified), cases cited: EB-2023-0195, EB-2026-0164_

Both utilities forecast a small first-year change in the total bill for a typical residential customer. Toronto Hydro's is an increase of 0.7%, and Hydro One's depends on which part of its application you look at. The two aren't directly comparable.

**Toronto Hydro (first test year 2025, 750 kWh/month)**
- **Total bill:** up $0.91 per 30 days, or 0.7%, after taxes and rebate [EB-2023-0195 / Exhibit 8 / p.13].
- **Distribution portion only:** up $3.24 per 30 days, or 7.6% [EB-2023-0195 / Exhibit 8 / p.13]. Distribution charges rise from the 2025 base to $45.93 a month [EB-2023-0195 / Exhibit 1B / p.41].
- **Later years:** the total-bill increases are $3.44 in 2026, $3.77 in 2027, $3.61 in 2028 and $2.90 in 2029, which is about 2% a year [EB-2023-0195 / Exhibit 8 / p.13].

**Hydro One (first test year 2028, typical R1 residential at 750 kWh/month)**
Hydro One files transmission and distribution separately, so there is no single combined figure.
- **Transmission:** the total monthly bill rises $0.50, or 0.3%, in 2028 [EB-2026-0164 / Exhibit A / p.27].
- **HOSSM (the other transmission entity, filed in the same application):** the 2028 change is $0.02, or 0.02% [EB-2026-0164 / Exhibit A / p.27].
- **Distribution with Distribution Rate Protection (DRP):** the total bill falls $3.31, or 2.1%, in 2028 [EB-2026-0164 / Exhibit A / p.28]. DRP limits increases in base distribution charges to inflation [EB-2026-0164 / Exhibit A / p.253].
- **Distribution without DRP:** the first-year increase is $13.56, or 7% of the total bill [EB-2026-0164 / Exhibit A / p.253]. The filing says these numbers depend on Hydro One's 2027 annual update, which could raise the Year 1 impacts [EB-2026-0164 / Exhibit A / p.253].

**Comparability**
- **Scope:** Toronto Hydro is a distribution-only application, so its 0.7% total-bill figure covers distribution. Hydro One's figures are split between transmission and distribution.
- **Years:** Toronto Hydro's first year is 2025, and Hydro One's is 2028.
- **Protection:** Hydro One's distribution result depends heavily on DRP, which accounts for the gap between a 2.1% decrease and a 7% increase.
- **Adding the pieces:** I did not add Hydro One's transmission and distribution figures into one number. The filing doesn't give a combined figure, and the bases may differ.

## cmp-6. How does each utility describe the effect of electrification and load growth on its investment plan?

_Tool calls: 2, citations: 11 (0 unverified), cases cited: EB-2023-0195, EB-2026-0164_

Both utilities say electrification and load growth shape their plans, but they treat it differently. Toronto Hydro builds the capacity in advance. Hydro One responds as the load appears.

**Toronto Hydro (EB-2023-0195, 2025–2029)**
- **Overall approach:** It calls its approach "least regrets" and says it must be "measured-but-proactive". Asset and human-capital investments have long lead times, so it wants to be ready to serve in all scenarios. [EB-2023-0195 / Exhibit 1B / p.19]
- **Peak demand:** The investment plan accommodates a 23% increase in system peak demand. That increase includes EV electrification, major transit projects (Ontario Line, Scarborough Subway Extension) and redevelopment of Downsview, the Port Lands and the Green Mile. [EB-2023-0195 / Exhibit 1B / p.19]
- **Programs affected:** It groups growth and electrification spending into these programs. [EB-2023-0195 / Exhibit 1B / p.20]
  - Customer Connections: $476M
  - Stations Expansion: $173M
  - Load Demand: $236M
  - Externally Initiated Plant Relocations & Expansions: $76M
  - Generation Protection, Monitoring and Control: $35M
  - Non-Wires Solutions: $23M
- **Heat pumps:** The peak forecast does not include wide-scale building electrification, because policy and consumer-behaviour drivers are uncertain. [EB-2023-0195 / Exhibit 2B / p.88] It did not model heat electrification as its own driver because of uncertainty about heat-pump uptake. [EB-2023-0195 / Exhibit 1B / p.352]
- **Stress test:** It used a Future Energy Scenarios study to test whether the capacity plan could handle a faster winter-peak build-up. It concluded the plan can, so the investments are "least regrets". It still expects to remain summer-peaking in 2025–2029, though the scenarios suggest it becomes winter-peaking in the 2030s. [EB-2023-0195 / Exhibit 2B / p.88]
- **Isolating EV effects:** It says it cannot isolate EVs' effect on spending. The peak forecast and investment plan are an aggregate view, and EVs are one of several drivers. [EB-2023-0195 / Exhibit 1B / p.352]
- **Risk mechanism:** Localized constraints could still arise from faster electrification. It therefore proposes a Demand Related Variance Account to track actual versus forecast demand-driven costs and revenues. [EB-2023-0195 / Exhibit 2B / p.88] [EB-2023-0195 / Exhibit 1B / p.20]

**Hydro One (EB-2026-0164, 2028–2032)**
- **Overall approach:** Electrification is a key consideration in distribution planning. It is built in through load forecasting, load-growth investment planning, design practices for new connections and service upgrades, and service-transformer assessments. The stated aim is to respond where impacts emerge and avoid building capacity "materially in advance of need". [EB-2026-0164 / Exhibit B / p.693]
- **Load forecast:** The forecast includes electrification alongside economic and demographic drivers. [EB-2026-0164 / Exhibit B / p.693]
- **Capacity investments:** EV and heat-pump adoption is incorporated into the load forecasts behind capacity-driven investments. These are D-SS-01 System Upgrades Driven by Load Growth and G-GP-22 Distribution Capital Contribution to Hydro One Transmission. [EB-2026-0164 / Exhibit B / p.694]
- **Connections and service upgrades:** Hydro One reports that EV chargers and heat pumps are driving more service upgrades. Volumes are expected to keep growing over 2028–2032. It has updated its design practices to size new assets for higher residential load. [EB-2026-0164 / Exhibit B / p.694]
- **Secondary assets:** It uses the EPRI Hotspotter tool to estimate secondary transformers that EV penetration will overload in 2028–2032. [EB-2026-0164 / Exhibit B3 / p.1430]
- **Long-term study (ERM, 2030–2050):** This study was commissioned under an EB-2021-0110 settlement commitment. [EB-2026-0164 / Exhibit B / p.220]
  - Network peak load rises from 8 GW today to as much as 15 GW by 2050.
  - Heat electrification is the largest long-term driver.
  - The network is expected to shift from summer-peaking to winter-peaking within the next decade. [EB-2026-0164 / Exhibit B3 / p.483]
  - The study excluded single large sites such as data centres, and it did not cost network reinforcement. [EB-2026-0164 / Exhibit B / p.220] [EB-2026-0164 / Exhibit B3 / p.440]
  - It also points to non-wires solutions to defer or avoid reinforcement where cost-effective. [EB-2026-0164 / Exhibit B / p.690]

**Where they aren't directly comparable**
- Toronto Hydro is an urban distributor and gives a single plan-wide figure, a 23% peak increase. Hydro One's figures come from a long-range scenario study (to 2050) that is separate from its planning forecast. [EB-2026-0164 / Exhibit B / p.220]
- Toronto Hydro excludes wide-scale heat electrification from its base forecast. Hydro One says heat pumps are in its load forecasts. [EB-2023-0195 / Exhibit 2B / p.88] [EB-2026-0164 / Exhibit B / p.694]
- I did not find a single dollar figure for electrification's effect on Hydro One's plan. The search results for its D-SS-01 and D-SA-02 programs didn't show dollar amounts. I can look those up if you want them.
